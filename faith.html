A Plain Account for the Newcomer and the Curious

We believe in Knowledge.

Not a god who holds it. Not a spirit who grants it. Knowledge itself is the thing pursued, the thing recorded, the thing passed from hand to hand across generations, treated as sacred in its own right.

This is not a faith of mystery. It does not ask you to accept what cannot be verified, nor to kneel before what cannot be questioned. It asks only that you reach honestly, consistently, and without pretending to hold what you do not yet have.

What We Believe

We believe in Knowledge.

Not a god who holds it. Not a spirit who grants it. Knowledge itself is the thing pursued, the thing recorded, the thing passed from hand to hand across generations and treated as sacred in its own right.

This is not a faith of mystery. It does not ask you to accept what cannot be verified, nor to kneel before what cannot be questioned. It asks only that you reach honestly, consistently, and without pretending to hold what you do not yet have.

Who Jonas Falx Was

Jonas Falx was a man.

This must be said first, and said plainly, because every error the old church made began the day it forgot this sentence.

He was a man who pursued Knowledge the way another man pursues bread without ceasing, and without asking whether he had earned the right to hunger for it. He built the Great Machine with his own hands, in his own age, at a cost no one had the right to ask of him and that he paid regardless. He did not do it for glory. He did not ask to be worshipped for it.

He is not our god. He is our measure.

Stand before him and know the distance between what you have given and what he gave. Let that distance be neither comfort nor despair, only the truth, honestly kept.

What We Ask of You

The Faith is held up by Three Pillars. Remove any one of them and what remains is not the Faith, only a piece of it, which is not the same thing.

Faith is the belief that an answer exists before you hold it in your hand. It is what keeps you searching on the day the search feels like foolishness. Without it, the work becomes despair.

Discipline is the daily labor of study, record, and practice, sustained past the hour when it stops feeling like joy and becomes only work. Without it, belief becomes appetite, and appetite is not devotion.

Worship is the care taken with Knowledge once it is found. A thing handled only for its use, without reverence, is eventually handled carelessly. What is handled carelessly is eventually lost. Without it, true things fade until no one notices they are gone.

Jonas Falx gave all three, completely, without setting any of them down to rest the others. The Faith asks you to do the same. It will cost you. There is no version of it that does not.

What We Do Not Ask of You

We do not ask you to gather at fixed hours to prove your devotion by attendance.

The smith at his forge, striking true until the blade holds an edge it did not have that morning, has worshipped. The scholar who writes down one true thing she did not know the day before has prayed. The healer who records what worked and what did not, honestly, without softening the failures, has kept the Faith.

Work honestly done is worship. This Church has never asked its faithful to put down their tools to pick up a prayer.

We do not ask you to accept what cannot be verified. We ask you to hold what is believed as believed, and what is known as known, and never to let the two change places for comfort's sake.

We do not ask you to call Jonas Falx a god. We ask you not to. He wore the name others put on him as a man wears a coat two sizes too large, not because it fit, but because refusing it in the moment it was offered seemed a cruelty he could not bring himself to commit. He said he wanted none of it. The New Church believes him. The old church did not, and you have seen what the old church built.

What We Will Not Tolerate

The Faith is not lax because it does not bind your hours. It is not gentle with its heresies.

To raise any person to godhood, however beloved, however learned, however much a congregation wishes to kneel, is the error that destroyed the church before this one. It will not be repeated here, whoever commits it, and whatever standing they held before they did.

To record what comfort requires instead of what is verified is to commit the same error with a pen instead of a throne. The hand that writes a convenient story instead of a true one has already begun building the idol.

To stop seeking is to let Knowledge withdraw. This is not a sin in the old sense. It is only the plain result of a hand that has stopped reaching for what it claims to want.

About Heresy

Being a heretic is not punishable in itself unless it is damaging to us or to the Jonasite faith. Visitors may be called heretics, but they should not take offense to it. It is often a statement of disagreement, not a threat. We do not punish honest disagreement for its own sake. We punish damage, deception, and harm.

In Temporis, a visitor may be corrected, challenged, or spoken against in the blunt language of conviction without offense being taken as a personal attack. The Faith does not demand submission to every opinion, but it does demand a standard of honesty and restraint, especially where harm, manipulation, or falsehood would be caused.

How to Worship

Keep a daily accounting. At the end of each day, write down what you actually learned, actually did, and actually verified, not what you meant to do, not what would comfort you to believe you did. This is the whole of the Faith's core practice. It is not complicated. It is only hard.

Come to the cathedral when the year has been long. Not because attendance is required, but because a place exists to bring your accounting when you need to stand before the standard and be honest about the distance still uncrossed.

Learn something true today that you did not know yesterday. Share it. Record it. Protect it. That is worship.

A Final Word

This Faith does not promise peace. It does not promise that Knowledge, honestly pursued, will be comfortable to hold or safe to share or rewarded in your lifetime.

Jonas Falx pursued it completely, and was misread by the people around him for it, and died as men die without seeing the full consequence of what he built.

What the Faith promises is this: that what is honestly known, honestly kept, and honestly passed on does not disappear. It outlasts the person who found it. It outlasts the age that tried to bury it. It outlasts the rot.

Reach. Record. Give the knowledge you find to the next hand that needs it.

That is the whole of it.

The Book of Jonas

Being the First Testament of the Church of Jonas Falx, copied and kept in the Archive of Temporis under the hand of Calem Rhovan, High Archivist.

Chapter One

1. Now there was a house built on blades, and its lord was Ingmar Falx, who had won his fiefdom by fire and the favor of the Emperor.

2. And to Ingmar a son was born, and his name was Jonas.

3. But Jonas was not as his father. Where Ingmar loved the taking of things, Jonas loved the understanding of them, and from his youngest years he could be found not on the training yard but in the library, turning pages by candlelight until the wax pooled cold at his elbow.

4. And it came to pass that a boy of the village crept into the estate by night, meaning to steal bread and coin, and his name was Tobias.

5. Tobias came upon Jonas in the dark of the library, and was afraid, for he thought himself caught and doomed.

6. But Jonas did not call the guards. Jonas looked upon the frightened boy and said, "You are not the only one hiding from this house."

7. And so a friendship was forged that no blade of Ingmar's could have cut, for Jonas taught Tobias his letters, and Tobias taught Jonas the forests and the wild paths his father's walls had never let him see.

8. And the two became as brothers, though one was lordborn and one was not, for knowledge does not ask after blood.

Chapter Two

1. In time, old Ingmar died, and Jonas became lord in his place. And Tobias, once a thief in the dark, walked now beside him in the light, as friend and assistant both.

2. Together they went to Cade Hill, to the university, that Jonas might learn all that was known, and then go looking for what was not.

3. And in his workshop, alone through many nights, Jonas labored over a device the like of which no scholar had built before, nor has any built since in truth.

4. He called it the Lens.

5. And when at last he raised it to his eye, he did not see the dark of his own workshop. He saw another world entire, one that shone like scattered gemstones, full of shapes both mechanical and strangely, terribly alive.

6. Jonas could not walk into that world. He could only watch. But watching was enough, for a mind like his could take a single glimpse and turn it into a hundred inventions.

7. And Tobias, watching him watch, said, "What is it you see?"

8. And Jonas answered, low, as a man speaking of something holy, "I see how it is all built. I only need to learn how to build it here."

Chapter Three

1. From the visions of the Lens, Jonas raised up wonders, and he gave them not to kings but to the people.

2. He made small skittering creatures of brass and gear to dig where a man's back would break, and the miners came to love them as pets, and named them locusts for their jittering legs.

3. He made a worm-shaped thing that could carry a voice across great distance and cry out when the tunnels groaned before a collapse, and he called it the bell, and the workmen kept it near as a charm against the dark.

4. He made a colossus taller than three men bound together, to lift what no crowd of hands could lift, and when it first rose and took its first step, the watching crowd screamed and scattered until it knelt, gently, and helped an old woman to her feet, and then the crowd did not scream. They cheered.

5. And all these things were built from a power Jonas drew from the world beyond the Lens, a glowing substance he named prima materia, and by it Tobias too became something more than a scholar's assistant, something the old tongues would later call a sorcerer.

6. Now the highborn did not know what to make of Jonas, for he was of noble house yet humble of manner, and this offended some among them.

7. But the lowborn came to love him, for his machines eased the ache in their backs and the fear in their nights, and it is remembered that even those who despised his blood could not despise his hands.

Chapter Four

1. But there came a darkness upon the fields that no plough could turn and no prayer could lift, and the people called it the Rot.

2. A scholar named Demetri Arvo was sent to look upon it, and he went with scorn in his heart, thinking it a peasant's exaggeration and himself too great a mind to be troubled long by mud and blight.

3. He returned a different man. He wrote, "I vacillate between fury and flight. What dreadful scourge is this?" And he never fully recovered his composure again.

4. And when word reached Jonas of the Rot, it is remembered that his face went pale as candle wax, and he said nothing, and he vanished into his workshop for a season, emerging only for bread and water.

5. For he alone among all the scholars of the Empire suspected, though he told no one, not even Tobias, that something in his own reaching toward that other world may have cracked a door that should have stayed shut.

6. And this is remembered in the Archive as the first hard truth of the Book: that the hands which build the ladder do not always know what climbs it after them.

Chapter Five

1. When the Rot had swallowed field after field and the wise men of the Empire had exhausted their wisdom and found only silence, the Emperor called all the great minds of the land together, and this council was named the Diet of Kings.

2. And in that hall, no one spoke, for every mouth that opened to offer hope had already been proven a liar by the Rot.

3. Then Jonas rose.

4. He did not soften what he had to say. He named the plague plainly before them all: desolation made manifest, he called it, and he showed them upon his parchments how it ate a body from the skin inward until nothing of a person remained but silence.

5. But having spoken the whole truth of the horror, he did not sit back down as a lesser man might, content merely to have been the one brave enough to say it aloud.

6. He said, "I have not come only to name your grief. I have come to offer you a way through it."

7. And he unveiled before them his design for a Great Machine, one that he believed could tear a door to a world untouched by Rot, and he asked of the kings only that they give their people to the work of building it.

8. And it is said that not every king swore the oath that day. Some clung instead to their gold and their crumbling lands, and history remembers them only as the lords of rubble.

Chapter Six

1. So the peoples of the Empire went down into the dark together, noble and common alike, for the Rot did not care for rank, and so, for the first time in living memory, neither did the labor.

2. They dug. They starved. They built. And as they built, they came to love Jonas as men love something they do not fully understand but desperately need to be true.

3. It is remembered that the hungry offered him their last crusts of bread though he never asked it of them, and wept simply at the sight of his face passing among the tunnels.

4. And Jonas, seeing this, grew troubled rather than proud, and confided to no one but his private journal, "They weep for joy and offer me what meager supplies they have. I am no god. I am only tired, like they are."

5. Even his own creations, in that long dark season, began to change. The locusts, the bells, the great walking colossi growing strange and unfamiliar to the man who had made them, as though something of that other, gemstone-bright world were beginning to lean back through the door he had cracked open.

6. And Tobias, his dearest friend, was struck down by the Rot in those final months, and was sent to the Lazaret to wait out his death in the dark, as thousands of nameless others had waited before him.

7. Jonas could not save him. Jonas could only finish the work.

Chapter Seven

1. And on the day the Great Machine at last woke, the earth itself convulsed as though the world were drawing one great breath, and in an instant the Rot was torn wholly from the land.

2. But all who stood nearest the Machine were torn away with it, not destroyed, but taken, pulled beyond the world entire into someplace no scripture before this one has ever finished describing.

3. Tobias, too far away in the Lazaret to be taken, was instead simply made whole, his Rot cleansed by the same great breath, though half his body remained forever marked by what it had already stolen from him before the miracle arrived.

4. And of Jonas himself, nothing more is known. He was near the heart of the Machine when it woke. He did not walk out of that dark again.

5. So it is written, and so the Church has always taught: Jonas Falx did not ascend to godhood, and did not choose to vanish as a martyr chooses his ending. He was simply a man, standing too close to the door he himself had built, on the one day it finally opened all the way.

6. This is why the Church that bears his name has never once called him divine, though five centuries of grieving, hoping people have wanted badly to make him one. He is kept, deliberately, as a man, for a man is something the rest of us might still become, and a god is only something we are asked to kneel before.

Chapter Eight

1. Long after, in the Empire that rose from the ash of that world remade, one stood as High Archivist over the Church of Jonas Falx and the great weight of everything it had chosen to remember. His name was Calem Rhovan.

2. And it fell to him, as it fell to no council, no twin seat, no shared crown before him, to carry the whole of the Archive largely alone with only a small core of the faithful still believing beside him.

3. Calem did not seek this weight any more than Jonas had sought worship. But weight does not ask before it settles, and a man who has spent his life among old ink and older grief learns to carry what is given him, because someone must, and the alternative is that it is simply lost.

4. Let it be recorded, then, at the closing of this First Book, that the line does not end with Jonas, nor with Tobias who kept his memory alive across five hundred lonely years, nor with whoever first copied these words onto vellum by candlelight.

5. It continues in every Archivist who chooses, on a given morning, to open the old books instead of letting them go quiet.

Authorized under Calem Rhovan, High Archivist of the Church of Jonas Falx.
